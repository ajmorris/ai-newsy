Nonprofit that tracks meteors taken down by "critical blow" from a cyberattack

The International Meteor Organization (IMO), a nonprofit that coordinates amateur and professional meteor observations since 1988, was hit by a cyberattack that dealt a 'critical blow' to its aging infrastructure. The attack took much of its website offline, and the organization expects several weeks of partial downtime as it transitions to new infrastructure. No group has claimed responsibility, and no ransom demand has been reported. The IMO is prioritizing fireball observation reporting during the outage and sharing updates via Facebook. The motive is unclear, as the organization's data is mostly public.

Why it matters

This attack highlights how vulnerable small nonprofits with aging infrastructure can be — they often lack the budget and staffing for robust cybersecurity, making them soft targets even when there's no obvious financial or strategic motive for attackers. It's a dispiriting reminder that threat actors don't always follow rational cost-benefit logic; sometimes organizations get hit simply because they're exposed. Iceland-based Treble raises $18 million for its voice simulation platform

Iceland-based startup Treble has raised $18 million in a Series A extension led by Paladin Capital Group, bringing its total funding to over $40 million. Founded in 2020 by acoustic engineers Finnur Pind and Jesper Pedersen, Treble builds a voice simulation platform used by voice AI model developers, AI wearable companies, and robotics firms. The platform offers synthetic data generation for speech enhancement and noise suppression, evaluates voice AI models under various conditions, and provides virtual prototyping for hardware companies designing headphones, speakers, smart glasses, and other devices. Treble counts Amazon and Logitech among its customers and recently partnered with Hugging Face to launch a speech recognition benchmark. The company plans to expand into physical AI applications including robotics, automotive, and drones.

Why it matters

Zuck sits out the AI slowdown

Mark Zuckerberg has publicly broken ranks with the coordinated AI slowdown proposed by Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei, arguing that each lab already has its own responsibility and incentive to pace itself safely. Zuckerberg pointed to Meta's voluntary safety hold on its new Muse AI agent as evidence that companies self-regulate without needing a coordinated pause. He framed alignment as a competitive selling point rather than a collective obligation, stating that any lab skipping safety 'will fall behind.' The slowdown debate now splits with Amodei, Altman, Musk, and Hassabis favoring coordination, while Zuckerberg, Jensen Huang, Trump, and Beijing oppose it. The article frames this as a Prisoner's Dilemma where defection by major players makes acceleration the likely default outcome.

Why it matters

Zuckerberg's position is strategically self-serving but contains a kernel of truth: market incentives do push labs toward safer products, since users genuinely don't want unreliable or dangerous AI agents. However, his argument conveniently ignores that the most dangerous capabilities — recursive self-improvement, autonomous weapon applications, mass surveillance — are not the kind of risks that market forces correct for, because the harm falls on society rather than on customers. The 2.5-hour AI-generated Odyssey movie is 2.5 hours too long

The Verge reviews 'Odysseus: The Fall,' a 2.5-hour AI-generated film by Fountain 0 that retells Homer's Odyssey. Written and directed by company cofounder Ash Koosha, who also provided his likeness for the title character and created the music, it is billed as the first AI-generated film at Hollywood blockbuster level. The reviewer, Andrew Webster, found it far from that claim, describing it as plagued by fundamental issues and progressively getting worse throughout its runtime. The film is available to rent for $9.99 via web browser only, and its release coincides with Christopher Nolan's successful traditional adaptation of The Odyssey.

Why it matters

The reviewer is overwhelmingly negative, calling the film 'so bad that it might just make viewers hate the original tale altogether' and describing it as '2.5 hours too long.' Andrew Webster characterizes it as the opposite of a promising glimpse at AI filmmaking's future, instead suggesting it raises doubts about whether AI filmmaking has any future at all. The review frames the film as ill-advised slop that fails to deliver on its ambitious billing as a Hollywood-level production.
