A Marc Benioff-backed startup thinks AI can solve the AI deployment problem

The Rundown AI

OpenAI's 'Astra' solves 10 long-standing math problems

OpenAI revealed that an internal unreleased model called 'Astra' has solved 10 long-standing open problems in mathematics and computer science, including problems in geometry, group theory, and quantum complexity. Notable achievements include proving the existence of non-sofic groups (open since 1999), solving Alain Connes's rigidity conjecture, Ehrhart's volume conjecture, and clearing three problems from Paul Erdős's list. All proofs have been verified in the Lean proof assistant, with chain-of-thought walkthroughs released, and the total compute cost was approximately $2,000 in API tokens. Within 24 hours, Anthropic claimed its model 'Fable' could reproduce five of the 10 proofs. The development has sparked debate about whether machine-generated proofs could be worthy of Fields Medal recognition.

Why it matters

If these claims hold up under scrutiny, this represents a genuinely historic moment in mathematics and AI. The verification in Lean is crucial — it means these aren't hallucinated proofs but formally verified results, which is a much higher bar than typical AI benchmarks. The fact that problems open for decades were solved at a cost of roughly $2,000 is staggering and suggests we may be entering an era where computational brute-force reasoning can systematically attack open conjectures. Is paying artists enough to convince them to embrace AI?

The article discusses Pippa, a generative AI startup that differentiates itself by paying artists directly through a revenue share system. Unlike other AI companies that have trained models on artists' work without permission—a practice many illustrators consider tantamount to theft—Pippa compensates artists every time a subscriber generates content whose style is derived from their work. The company's cofounders, Hogan Shrum and Sean Wright, believe this artist-focused approach sets them apart in the text-to-video AI space. The article explores whether financial compensation is sufficient to convince artists to embrace AI technology, given the years of contentious legal battles and ethical concerns surrounding unauthorized use of creative work for AI training.

Why it matters

This is a genuinely important question that gets at the heart of the AI-art debate. While paying artists is clearly better than not paying them, compensation alone likely isn't enough to address all concerns. The fundamental issues go beyond money—they include questions of consent, creative control, the potential devaluation of artistic labor, and the existential threat many artists feel about being replaced by tools trained on their own work.
