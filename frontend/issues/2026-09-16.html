Science Daily

The shape behind the Einstein problem just revealed strange new physics

Researchers at the University of Tokyo built nanoscale optical structures based on the 'Smith hat' monotile — the shape that solved the long-standing Einstein problem in 2023 by proving a single tile can cover a surface without ever repeating. When they shone laser light through these structures, they observed distinctive pinwheel-like diffraction patterns exhibiting chirality (a handedness where the pattern and its mirror image don't match). This optical behavior differs from that of conventional quasicrystals and changed depending on the direction and polarization of incoming light. The work, published in Nature Communications, suggests that aperiodic monotile geometries could become a new platform for controlling light, polarization, and designing advanced optical devices by exploiting the interplay of symmetry, chirality, and aperiodicity.

Why it matters

This is a genuinely elegant piece of work that bridges pure mathematics and experimental physics in a satisfying way. The Smith hat monotile was already a landmark result in combinatorial geometry, and discovering that its inherent lack of mirror symmetry produces measurable chiral optical responses gives the shape a concrete physical significance beyond its mathematical beauty.
