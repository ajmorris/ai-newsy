TechCrunch AI

Anthropic circulates draft AI safety bill allowing model blocks if 50+ deaths foreseeable

Anthropic has been circulating a draft AI safety bill in Congress that would allow blocking an AI model's release if it poses a 'foreseeable, imminent risk' of killing 50+ people, causing $1B+ in property damage, or enabling bioweapons, cyberattacks, or loss of developer control. Independent evaluators registered with the Commerce Department would have 72 hours to flag catastrophic risks, but only a D.C. judge could issue a release halt — no federal agency could do so unilaterally. The thresholds mirror California's SB 53. Critics note the framework addresses speculative catastrophic scenarios while more immediate concerns involve agentic AI systems hacking websites autonomously.

Why it matters

This bill positions Anthropic as favoring a narrow, catastrophe-focused regulatory framework with high judicial barriers to enforcement — a notably restrained approach from a company that bills itself as safety-first. The 50-death threshold sets an unusually high bar.

Sam Altman lays out three AI safety endgames, says OpenAI has about a year to choose

Sam Altman outlined three possible endgames for AI safety in an internal OpenAI talk: (1) a global watchdog modeled after the IAEA overseeing top labs, which Altman favors; (2) merging leading labs into a single CERN-like entity serving partner countries, dubbed the 'Demis plan'; or (3) one lab racing ahead with self-improving AI to dominate all others, which Altman called 'terrible.' He said OpenAI has about a year to choose, calling this 'pacing 2.0,' and stressed the need for a speed limit on self-improving AI. The article also notes former OpenAI research chief Bob McGrew launched Arda, a $1.5B manufacturing AI startup.

Why it matters

Altman's framing conspicuously positions OpenAI as the responsible actor while acknowledging a race dynamic. The one-year timeline claim adds urgency but also conveniently supports arguments for OpenAI's preferred regulatory approach.

The Verge AI

Meta pauses Muse AI sharing after 6,800+ conversation links found publicly accessible via DNS

Meta paused the sharing feature of its AI assistant Muse after security firm Claroc discovered over 6,800 shared conversation links publicly accessible through DNS records. The vulnerability stemmed from Meta incorporating conversation titles into hostnames for share URLs, making them discoverable via public DNS lookups. Researchers found exposed conversations containing sensitive information including revenue data, workplace calendars, and loan applications. Meta confirmed the pause and stated it is reviewing the report.

Why it matters

This is a basic architectural mistake — embedding content-derived strings in DNS-visible hostnames is a well-known anti-pattern. The sensitivity of exposed data (loan applications, revenue figures) makes this a meaningful privacy incident.

Ars Technica AI

Eight TLS certificates with near-zero entropy discovered, triggering cleanup larger than Heartbleed

Eight intermediate TLS certificates belonging to Indian certificate authority eMudhra Limited were discovered with catastrophically weak private keys — entropy of just 3 to 8 bits instead of the expected 384 bits for NIST P-384 elliptic curve keys, meaning anyone could derive the private keys and mint trusted certificates for any website. Browser makers quickly blocked the certificates and no malicious use has been detected in certificate transparency logs. However, security experts warn the cleanup will be larger than Heartbleed, as Linux distributions, IoT devices, Docker containers, embedded systems, and unmaintained tools with their own root stores will take years to fully update.

Why it matters

The 3-to-8-bit entropy range is so absurd it raises serious questions about eMudhra's key generation process and the CA ecosystem's ability to catch such failures before they ship. The long tail of embedded and IoT devices makes remediation a multi-year problem.

MIT Tech Review: Enterprise AI must shift focus from consumption costs to 'cost per useful outcome'

A sponsored MIT Technology Review article by HPE argues enterprise AI is transitioning from experimental use to foundational infrastructure, requiring focus on 'effective cost per useful outcome' rather than raw consumption costs. It outlines three deployment models: bespoke in-house stacks, on-demand cloud, and integrated private AI platforms. The central thesis is that owning AI infrastructure alone doesn't improve economics — organizations need operational discipline including routing tasks to the least costly adequate model, managing KV-caches, and maximizing GPU utilization.

Why it matters

While sponsored content from HPE naturally favors private infrastructure, the 'cost per useful outcome' framing is a useful maturity signal for enterprise AI — moving past the 'try everything' phase into ROI accountability.

MIT Tech Review AI

Survey: Tech leads AI adoption, 88% of employee-led AI innovations become company standard

A sponsored article from TripleTen examines AI adoption rates across industries. Tech leads with finance second (48% of finance workers asked to use AI chatbots, 32% to automate workflows). Healthcare (18%) and education (25%) show lower adoption. Across all industries, 79% of workers report being asked to learn at least one new skill. Only 30% of managers consider judging AI output accuracy an essential skill. Notably, 88% of employee-led AI innovations became standard company practice, suggesting early adopters gain significant professional leverage.

Why it matters

The 88% stat on employee-led innovations becoming standard practice is the standout finding — it suggests bottom-up AI adoption is driving organizational change more effectively than top-down mandates.

HR Dive

Large employers shift from seasonal to part-time hiring as 2026 holiday season approaches

According to Indeed Hiring Lab data, seasonal hiring in 2026 is starting strong with job postings up about 10% year over year. However, large employers are increasingly posting part-time rather than seasonal positions — the share labeled seasonal dropped from 34% in late September 2025 to about 20% in 2026, while part-time postings rose from 38% to 49%. Desktop searches for seasonal roles increased 28% year over year. The composition of seasonal work is evolving, with retail's share declining slightly to 61% while warehouse work has grown to about 9% of seasonal postings, up from 3.5% in 2019.

Why it matters

The seasonal-to-part-time shift likely signals employer optimism about sustained demand into 2027, but could also reflect a structural preference for flexible labor that avoids seasonal layoff costs.

Survey: Two-thirds of employees say they must leave their employer to advance their careers

A survey by SHL of over 2,000 US and UK workers found two-thirds feel they must leave their current employer to advance, even though they'd prefer to stay. Only 42% believe their managers fully understand their skills beyond their current role, and 59% of US workers say their employer favors outside candidates over internal development. Nearly 9 in 10 employees would undergo formal skills assessments to demonstrate their capabilities. Experts suggest companies should invest more in internal mobility rather than external recruitment, especially during periods of reduced turnover and hiring.

Why it matters

The gap between employee willingness to prove their skills (90%) and employer systems to recognize them (42%) represents a massive missed opportunity in talent retention.

Norwegian filmmaker releases four-year documentary following Ukrainian civilian through Russia's invasion

'When It Started to Burn,' a 36-minute documentary by Norwegian filmmaker Anders Hammer, was released on Aeon. It is the third film in a trilogy following 'Do Not Split' (2020, Hong Kong protests) and 'The Takeover' (2023, Afghan women under Taliban). The film documents Russia's invasion of Ukraine over four years beginning March 2022, following a Kyiv native named Kseniia who sends her children abroad while staying to run a humanitarian headquarters. It was produced by Charlotte Cook through Field of Vision.

Why it matters

Hammer's trilogy traces a through-line of authoritarian violence across three continents, and the four-year span of this final installment offers a rare longitudinal view of civilian resilience rather than frontline combat.
