Ars Technica AI

Critical 'sshole' SSH vulnerability in Linux under active exploitation

A critical vulnerability tracked as CVE-2026-123158, nicknamed 'sshole,' is being actively exploited to gain unauthenticated SSH access to privileged accounts on Linux systems. The flaw arises from the interaction between OpenSSH and the musl C library: when an attacker supplies a password longer than 256 characters for a passwordless account, musl's password hashing function erroneously returns an empty string that matches the wildcard stored in /etc/shadow. Alpine Linux is particularly affected. Security firms Greynoise, watchTowr, and Previdian have observed active exploitation including botnet activity and cryptocurrency mining.

Why it matters

This is a nasty interaction bug — neither OpenSSH nor musl is broken alone, but together they create an authentication bypass. Alpine Linux's prevalence in Docker containers means the blast radius is enormous, and many container images won't be patched quickly because they're built-and-forgotten.

MIT Tech Review AI

Data center backlash may be about electricity, not anti-tech populism

MIT Technology Review argues that the growing US backlash against data centers is fundamentally about electricity rather than a broader culture war against Big Tech. Reporting from Ashburn, Virginia — the world's largest data center hub — the article notes that data centers there average 54 megawatts each, more than Denver International Airport. Data centers face two problematic options: drawing from the grid and straining local infrastructure, or generating power onsite. New state rules in Virginia require data centers to pay for transmission line costs. Activists insist the fight has always been about power consumption, while industry figures like OpenAI's Chris Lehane frame opposition as a proxy for unease about corporate power.

Why it matters

This is a useful corrective to the narrative that data center opposition is purely ideological. When facilities consume more electricity than major airports, NIMBYism doesn't require any opinion about AI — the electric bill and grid strain are sufficient motivation.

Lenny's Newsletter

Tech job market reshuffles: PM roles surge 75% while engineering hiring stalls

Lenny Rachitsky's late 2026 tech job market report finds product manager openings up 75% from their March 2023 low, with PM's share of hiring growing from 16% to 21%. Engineering demand has stalled for the first time, with open roles growing just 2.5% this year and actual hiring down to about 113,000 annually — less than half the 2022 peak. Design roles are at a record low. AI skills are now table stakes across all roles. Tech jobs are concentrating in the Bay Area while remote work hits a record low. Tech recruiter openings are up nearly 300% since mid-2023, which could signal future reacceleration. Forward deployed engineer roles are also emerging as a growth area.

Why it matters

The PM surge alongside engineering stagnation suggests companies are shifting from building AI tools to figuring out what to do with them — a classic adoption-phase pattern. The recruiter spike is the most interesting signal: it's historically been a reliable 6-9 month leading indicator for broader hiring waves.

Guardian AI

Storyboard artists face existential threat as Hollywood adopts generative AI

AI adoption in Hollywood is threatening the jobs of storyboard artists — the illustrators who translate directors' visions into visual sequences before filming. While a fully AI-dominated film industry remains a future prospect, the immediate impact is already being felt by behind-the-scenes professionals, with storyboard artists among the most directly jeopardized by generative AI tools that can produce visual mockups quickly and cheaply.

Why it matters

Storyboard artists represent the pattern likely to repeat across creative industries: AI doesn't replace the headline talent but hollows out the skilled supporting roles that make production possible, concentrating both risk and cost savings on the people with the least leverage to resist.

Critics warn ChatGPT's Teen Mode may harm adolescent psychological development

Robin Kirman argues in The Guardian that OpenAI's Teen Mode for ChatGPT, which automatically enrolls users under 18 following a lawsuit alleging ChatGPT contributed to a teenager's suicide, may deprive teenagers of a fundamental developmental need: the ability to develop a reliable sense of their own minds. The piece examines how the tech industry has contested causal links between its products and harms cited in lawsuits, while suggesting that safety-focused modes may still impair young people's critical developmental experiences.

Why it matters

It's a genuinely interesting paradox: a safety mode designed to protect teens might itself cause a subtler harm by replacing the friction and ambiguity that adolescents need to develop independent judgment. Whether that's a real risk or philosophical hand-wringing depends entirely on usage intensity.
