MIT Tech Review AI

Samsung’s chip workers are jumping ship to rival SK Hynix

Samsung's semiconductor workers are increasingly leaving for rival SK Hynix, driven by a significant disparity in employee bonuses. SK Hynix is paying employees approximately $476,000 in bonuses this year, fueled by record profits from high-bandwidth memory (HBM) chips used in Nvidia's AI accelerators. Samsung's foundry division workers, whose unit operates at a loss, receive only about $135,000 in bonuses, while even Samsung's memory division workers get around $400,000. A Samsung labor union survey found that 81.5% of foundry division employees want to leave within two years, and over 200 union members have already departed for SK Hynix in recent months. The talent war between the two South Korean chipmakers is intensifying as both compete to dominate next-generation HBM chips critical to the AI boom.

Why it matters

This talent exodus highlights a critical vulnerability in Samsung's conglomerate structure, where division-level profit-sharing creates stark internal inequities that demoralize workers in underperforming units. The situation is particularly damaging because foundry engineers possess transferable skills valuable to competitors, and losing experienced talent could create a vicious cycle where Samsung's foundry division falls further behind, making it even harder to retain remaining staff. Anthropic’s Dario Amodei responds: doesn’t oppose open-weight models, but fears Chinese AI

Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei publicly clarified that his company has never advocated for banning open-weight AI models, responding to industry speculation. He stated that open-weight models without dangerous capabilities are a public good. However, he expressed fears about authoritarian governments, particularly China, building superior AI for military dominance or repression. He noted open-weight models are harder to apply guardrails to, making them riskier for enabling biological or cyber attacks. Amodei supports restricting China's chip access, cracking down on model distillation (IP theft), and creating an international model safety testing organization. His response came after Nvidia's Jensen Huang and other major AI companies published an open letter urging policymakers against premature restrictions on open-weight models.

Why it matters

This article captures a nuanced and politically significant moment in the AI industry's ongoing debate about openness versus safety. Amodei's position is carefully calibrated — he avoids alienating the open-source community while maintaining his safety-focused brand. His distinction between open-weight models as a 'public good' and the separate threat of authoritarian AI development is intellectually coherent but also conveniently positions Anthropic as both pro-innovation and pro-safety.
